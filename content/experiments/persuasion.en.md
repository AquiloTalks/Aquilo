## Kellynch Hall | Chapter 1

> Vanity was the beginning and the end of Sir Walter Elliot's character; vanity of person and of situation.

> ...her word had no weight, her convenience was always to give way—she was only Anne.

The book opens on the father's mirror, then on the daughter's silence. "Only Anne" puts the heroine where she can barely be heard. The whole novel is that voice slowly growing louder.

## The persuasion, eight years ago | Chapter 4

> She was persuaded to believe the engagement a wrong thing: indiscreet, improper, hardly capable of success, and not deserving it.

> She had been forced into prudence in her youth, she learned romance as she grew older: the natural sequel of an unnatural beginning.

The title lives here. Lady Russell is no villain; she truly wants the best for Anne, which is exactly why her advice is so hard to resist. Austen is not asking who was wrong, but what happens when someone without judgement of her own borrows another's.

## The return | Chapter 7

> Eight years, almost eight years had passed, since all had been given up.

> You were so altered he should not have known you again.

Wentworth comes back rich, admired and still angry. Mary passes the remark on carelessly, yet it is the most painful line in the book. Anne does not argue, because she thinks the same.

## A nut | Chapter 10

> Let those who would be happy be firm.

> ...a beautiful glossy nut, which, blessed with original strength, has outlived all the storms of autumn.

He is talking to Louisa, but every word lands on Anne, hidden behind the hedgerow. He holds firmness up as the highest virtue and reads Anne's old obedience as weakness. Two chapters later, Austen makes that firmness pay.

## The Cobb at Lyme | Chapter 12

> ...she was too precipitate by half a second, she fell on the pavement on the Lower Cobb, and was taken up lifeless!

> "Is there no one to help me?"

Louisa's firmness turns out to be wilfulness. In the panic, the first person to think clearly is Anne, the one supposedly too easily persuaded. For the first time Wentworth sees that firmness and stubbornness are not the same thing.

## Bath | Chapter 21

> Mr Elliot is a man without heart or conscience; a designing, wary, cold-blooded being, who thinks only of himself...

> Oh! he is black at heart, hollow and black!

This is the second persuasion. Lady Russell admires the polished Mr Elliot and hopes Anne will marry him. This time Anne is not pushed along: she watches, listens, and goes to her old friend Mrs Smith for the truth.

## The letter at the White Hart | Chapter 23

> All the privilege I claim for my own sex... is that of loving longest, when existence or when hope is gone.

> You pierce my soul. I am half agony, half hope.

Anne talks to Captain Harville, but speaks for Wentworth to hear; Wentworth writes a letter at the next table, for Anne to read. Each finally says, in their own way, what went unsaid for eight years. It is the quietest scene in the book, and the most intense.

## The ending | Chapters 23–24

> ...I was right in submitting to her, and that if I had done otherwise, I should have suffered more in continuing the engagement than I did even in giving it up...

> She gloried in being a sailor's wife, but she must pay the tax of quick alarm for belonging to that profession...

Anne does not say she was wrong; she says she had no better choice then. And the last line is not a fairy-tale "happily ever after" but happiness with worry inside it. Austen gives her love, and reality with it.
