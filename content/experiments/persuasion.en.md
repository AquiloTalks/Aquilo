## Only Anne

Sir Walter loves the man in the mirror and his title, and even deep in debt he only worries about losing face. Anne is the one clear-sighted person in the family, and the one with the least voice. The whole novel is that voice slowly growing louder.

> ...her word had no weight, her convenience was always to give way—she was only Anne.

## The persuasion, eight years ago

Lady Russell, who talks Anne out of the engagement, is no villain. She truly wants the best for her, which is what makes the advice so hard to resist. At nineteen Anne has no judgement of her own yet, so she takes someone else's for hers.

> She was persuaded to believe the engagement a wrong thing: indiscreet, improper, hardly capable of success, and not deserving it.

## The return

Wentworth comes back rich, admired and still angry. The cruellest line reaches Anne secondhand, passed on carelessly by her sister Mary. Anne does not argue, because she thinks the same.

> You were so altered he should not have known you again.

## The nut and the Cobb

Wentworth praises Louisa's firmness with a nut, and every word lands on Anne, hidden behind the hedgerow. Two chapters later Louisa insists on jumping from the steps of the Cobb and is knocked senseless. In the panic, the first person to think clearly is Anne, the one supposedly too easily persuaded. Firmness and stubbornness turn out to be different things.

> Let those who would be happy be firm.

## The second persuasion

In Bath, Lady Russell steers Anne's marriage once more, this time towards the polished Mr Elliot, heir to Kellynch. The same adviser, the same kind of persuasion, and this time Anne does not give way. She decides against him before she knows the truth about him, on her own judgement alone.

>> The persuasion, eight years ago

> She never could accept him. And it was not only that her feelings were still adverse to any man save one; her judgement... was against Mr Elliot.

## The letter

Anne tells Captain Harville that women love longest, and says it for Wentworth, writing at the next table, to hear. The letter he writes is for her to read. No embrace, no declaration face to face: eight years of silence end in one conversation and one letter.

> You pierce my soul. I am half agony, half hope.
